**RESEARCH HYPOTHESIS / THEORY ARTICLE**

**Restricted Autonomy, Stimulation Seeking, and Threat-Linked Relief**

*A Developmental Conditioning Hypothesis for Context-Dependent Attention Difficulties*

**Michael Zot**

Independent researcher \| 14 October 2026

Manuscript type: theoretical hypothesis and prospective research agenda

Unpublished manuscript; not peer reviewed. No original participant data or experimental results are reported.

# Abstract

Background: Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) has robust genetic and neurodevelopmental foundations, yet the expression of attention difficulties varies across contexts. Existing work links childhood adversity, restricted independent mobility, activity opportunities, and caregiver overprotection to aspects of self-regulation, but does not establish that limited childhood autonomy produces a specific learning sequence relevant to later attention symptoms. Objective: We propose Restricted Autonomy–Stimulation Conditioning (RASC), a developmental modifier hypothesis concerning repeated constraints on age-appropriate exploration and the consequences of obtaining relief through unauthorized freedom. Model: Recurrent autonomy restriction may generate under-stimulation and compensatory novelty seeking; access to freely chosen exploration may temporarily reduce restlessness; fear of rule violation may then pair the experience of relief with threat expectancy. With repetition, this context-specific learning could amplify existing ADHD traits or produce overlapping attention-related difficulties in a subgroup. Predictions: RASC anticipates disproportionate relief from choice beyond matched physical activity, subsequent rule-specific fear following unauthorized exploration, prospective exposure–response associations conditional on baseline symptoms, and preferential response to autonomy-supportive intervention. Methods proposed: A multi-wave developmental cohort, an ethically constrained within-person factorial experiment, and an active-control randomized trial are specified, with explicit confounding controls and disconfirming outcomes. Conclusion: RASC is a falsifiable, unvalidated explanatory proposal. The purpose is to isolate a potentially modifiable environmental pathway without redefining ADHD as entirely learned or suggesting replacement of established care.

Keywords: ADHD; autonomy restriction; independent mobility; self-regulation; reinforcement learning; fear conditioning; child development; hypothesis testing

# 1. Introduction

ADHD is characterized clinically by persistent patterns of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that interfere with functioning across relevant settings. The disorder has substantial genetic contributions and a large, replicated empirical literature; a major genome-wide association study identified 27 genome-wide significant loci \[1\], and an international consensus statement summarizes convergent evidence regarding diagnosis, course, and treatment \[2\]. A developmental account that ignores this evidence would be scientifically inadequate. At the same time, clinical symptom levels fluctuate with task characteristics, immediate rewards, perceived demands, stress, sleep, and activity context. Environmental variation in symptoms can therefore be investigated without disputing the validity of ADHD.

This article focuses on one under-specified environmental exposure: restricted autonomy, defined narrowly as a recurring lack of age-appropriate opportunities to choose, move, explore, and interact with peers, after accounting for real safety risks. Freedom of movement is not the same construct as neglect, maltreatment, or absence of loving care. In dangerous neighborhoods, protective restrictions may be rational, necessary, and beneficial in other respects. Their potential developmental costs, if any, remain an empirical matter.

The central proposal is that some children repeatedly encounter an asymmetric sequence: being confined creates an urge for stimulation; unauthorized exploration relieves that urge; anticipated punishment follows the relief. A sequence that repeatedly couples relief to threat could become a learned context-dependent regulator of behavior. We call this Restricted Autonomy–Stimulation Conditioning (RASC). This is offered as a theory with measurement and intervention implications, not as a newly discovered disease, a single-person diagnosis, or a demonstrated cause of ADHD.

# 2. The observation that generated the hypothesis

The motivating observation is a retrospective adult account of childhood in a setting where perceived external danger limited permission to spend time outdoors and socialize freely. During periods indoors, the individual recalled persistent efforts to invent stimulation and occasional departures without permission. Restlessness was remembered as diminishing rapidly while outside; anxiety about having broken a rule followed. The remembered pattern is therefore temporally ordered: restriction → activation and stimulation-seeking → self-directed escape → subjective relief → fear of consequences.

The observation matters because it identifies an unusual ordering of states that can be tested: relief first, threat-related fear second. It does not establish baseline childhood ADHD status, rule out recall bias, verify the frequency of events, or prove any enduring neurobiological adaptation. The account was neither collected under a structured clinical interview nor compared with a control group. Its role in this article is hypothesis generation only. Future studies should investigate the sequence prospectively with consent, neutral questioning, and external corroboration where possible.

# 3. Background and conceptual foundations

## 3.1. Genetic liability and phenotype heterogeneity

The RASC proposal is explicitly compatible with inherited ADHD liability. Genetic and environmental explanations need not be mutually exclusive: a child with high baseline activity drive may react more strongly to restricted movement, while the resulting behavior may lead caregivers to impose additional restrictions. This creates a feedback loop in which individual traits affect exposure and exposure potentially affects later behavior. It also means that an association between strict parenting and later attention symptoms cannot be interpreted as environmental causation without prospective information about earlier traits \[1–3\].

## 3.2. Childhood deprivation, threat, and their boundaries

The developmental literature distinguishes deprivation of expected environmental inputs from exposure to threat. A systematic review and meta-analysis linked both domains to differences in executive functioning, with deprivation showing stronger associations in some domains \[4\]. Related longitudinal work examined deprivation and threat as possible mediators of socioeconomic gradients in executive function \[5\]. Evidence from severe institutional deprivation additionally links extreme early experiences to later attention symptoms and electroencephalographic developmental measures \[6\]. These literatures demonstrate that environments matter, but they do not justify equating safety-driven restrictions within a caring household with severe institutional neglect.

A prospective systematic review concerning maltreatment and ADHD found associations but mixed evidence on temporal order: in some cohorts adversity preceded symptoms, whereas in others ADHD traits preceded adversity \[3\]. Restriction of outdoor play is not interchangeable with maltreatment; RASC accordingly treats the latter literature as mechanistic context, not direct confirmation.

## 3.3. Parental restriction may follow earlier symptoms

A longitudinal analysis by Meyer and colleagues found evidence consistent with early ADHD symptoms predicting later overprotective parenting, which in turn related to anxiety \[7\]. This creates an essential rival model: pre-existing restlessness and impulsivity might prompt caregivers to tighten rules, rather than the rules producing those behaviors. Any valid RASC test must assess baseline symptoms before exposure and estimate both directions rather than assuming a one-way causal chain.

## 3.4. Choice, independent mobility, and self-regulation

Self-determination theory identifies autonomy as a basic psychological need and predicts consequences for motivation when choices are constrained \[8\]. Such theory supplies conceptual plausibility, not evidence for the specific RASC sequence. Studies of children’s independent mobility demonstrate that neighborhood and family circumstances affect the opportunities children have to move outside without adult supervision \[9\]. This creates a plausible and ethically important distinction between objectively unsafe environments, caregivers’ perceptions of danger, and the actual autonomy a child experiences.

## 3.5. Movement, play, and attentional outcomes

A small quasi-experimental study of preschoolers compared executive functioning after outdoor versus indoor play \[10\]; randomized-trial syntheses have also reported executive-function benefits from physical-activity interventions in school-aged children with ADHD \[11\]. These findings strengthen a simple alternative: perhaps relief comes from exercise, novelty, or engagement rather than autonomy. RASC will add explanatory value only if the role of voluntary choice and punishment expectancy survives comparison with equally active, equally novel control conditions.

# 4. Proposed mechanism and causal structure

RASC consists of five linked constructs rather than one omnibus label. First, autonomy restriction reduces child-controlled opportunities for exploration and social contact. Second, a mismatch between desired and available stimulation increases subjective boredom or restlessness. Third, the child engages in compensatory activity such as mental novelty generation, high-frequency task switching, or unauthorized exploration. Fourth, self-selected exploration brings short-term relief. Fifth, anticipation of disciplinary consequences induces fear after or during that relief. Repetition could shape the learned expectation that externally controlled environments produce arousal, whereas self-directed activity brings regulation at a social cost.

The model is neither a confirmed neural circuit nor a claim that attention differences are caused by a simple reward deficit. It describes a set of observable transitions in context, behavior, and affect. Individual trajectories might differ: some children would show restriction-related restlessness without threat coupling; others would show anxiety without substantial attention effects; still others may already meet ADHD criteria irrespective of environmental restriction.

## 4.1. A minimal longitudinal formulation

At time t, let R denote the degree of autonomy restriction, S the child’s stimulation-seeking, F access to freely chosen activity, T threat expectancy, and Y later attention-related symptoms or functional impairment. A working structure is S(t+1) = f\[R(t), baseline traits, activity, context\]; relief(t) = g\[F(t), movement, novelty, expectation\]; T(t+1) = h\[rule violation, anticipated consequences, prior learning\]; and Y(t+1) = k\[prior Y, S, T, family risk, other developmental inputs\]. The functions are unspecified and the relations are hypothesized; the notation prevents the theory from being mistaken for an already estimated biological equation.

The proposed RASC effect is a conditional, incremental contribution to later symptoms or impairment. It is neither necessary nor sufficient for ADHD. Reverse paths are explicitly admitted: Y(t) may increase R(t+1) when caregivers respond to early symptoms. Threat can also increase restriction directly through actual neighborhood conditions. Successful research must separate these bidirectional and shared-cause pathways.

# 5. Operational definitions and measurement

A major weakness of many environmental accounts is vague labeling. RASC should be assessed through separate constructs with observable indicators and multiple reporters. There is presently no validated RASC instrument; naming these variables does not make a scale clinically usable.

| **Construct**              | **Suggested operational indicator**                                                                                             | **Key validity threat**                                                  |
|----------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Autonomy restriction       | Child-reported and caregiver-reported ability to choose outdoor time, destinations, peers, activities, and independent movement | Confusion with actual neighborhood danger or age-appropriate supervision |
| Under-stimulation          | Momentary boredom, restlessness, and urge to move during low-choice periods                                                     | General ADHD symptoms, fatigue, or insufficient sleep                    |
| Self-generated stimulation | Observed or logged task switching, novelty generation, exploratory behavior                                                     | Normal curiosity, creativity, developmental stage                        |
| Freedom-linked relief      | Within-person change in restlessness immediately following voluntary activity                                                   | Physical exertion, novelty, social contact, regression to mean           |
| Threat coupling            | Post-activity fear specifically tied to potential rule violation or punishment                                                  | Generalized anxiety or retrospective reinterpretation                    |
| Later outcomes             | Teacher and caregiver ratings, real-world impairment, independent attention tasks                                               | Rater expectation, diagnostic circularity, shared-method bias            |

A future RASC exposure inventory could ask separately what was allowed, what the child wanted to do, what risks were present, and what happened after unauthorized exploration. Item development should include cognitive interviews with children and caregivers from multiple cultural and safety contexts. Reliability, convergent and discriminant validity, measurement invariance, and test–retest stability would need formal evaluation. A “high-RASC subgroup” should never be declared using ad hoc cutoffs optimized on the same outcome data.

# 6. Distinguishing, falsifiable predictions

To qualify as a useful theory, RASC must make predictions that diverge from simpler accounts. The predictions below are proposed in advance of data collection; none is an empirical finding of this paper.

| **Code / prediction**             | **RASC-specific expectation**                                                                                                 | **Observation favoring rival model**                                                          |
|-----------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| P1. Choice effect                 | Restlessness falls more after child-chosen activity than equally engaging adult-assigned activity, holding movement constant. | Comparable relief under both choice conditions suggests movement or engagement is sufficient. |
| P2. Ordered emotional shift       | A subset shows immediate relief followed by fear specifically when activity is perceived as prohibited.                       | Fear unrelated to permission status suggests generalized anxiety.                             |
| P3. Temporal order                | Restriction predicts later symptom changes after adjusting for prior symptoms and major confounders.                          | Earlier symptoms predict later restriction, with no independent reverse path.                 |
| P4. Dose-response                 | Greater duration and severity of lost autonomy predicts stronger context sensitivity, conditional on genuine danger.          | No gradient after accounting for initial symptoms and family context.                         |
| P5. Intervention moderation       | People with prospectively established RASC markers benefit more from choice-rich than activity-matched programs.              | Identical effects across programs or groups.                                                  |
| P6. Incremental explanatory power | RASC measures add replicable predictive information beyond genetics proxies, baseline ADHD, anxiety, sleep, and activity.     | Out-of-sample predictive improvement is absent or negligible.                                 |
| P7. Generalization boundary       | Effects track actual restrictions and consequences across settings more closely than country or culture labels.               | Effects exist only with particular sample labels, suggesting confounding.                     |

# 7. Research program

## 7.1. Phase 0: Qualitative construct validation

Before testing causal predictions, recruit a purposive sample of caregivers, children, adolescents, and adults to evaluate whether the proposed five-stage sequence is recognizable and distinguishable from ordinary boredom, fear, and pre-existing hyperactivity. Use a preregistered interview guide and independent coders blinded to reported ADHD status. Probe counterexamples, including children confined for legitimate safety reasons who do not develop attention difficulties and children with ADHD who enjoyed unrestricted outdoor access. The principal deliverable is a revised construct map and item pool, not an estimate of causality.

## 7.2. Study A: Prospective developmental cohort

Design and population. A provisional target of approximately 1,200 children aged 5–8 years would be followed every six months over three years in communities differing in independently documented neighborhood safety. This is a planning illustration; final recruitment, clustering, and sample size must be justified with feasibility studies and power simulations. Recruitment should deliberately cover a wide range of permitted mobility rather than selecting only restrictive households.

Exposure and covariates. At each wave, record objectively constrained versus freely chosen activity, outdoor access, peer contact, caregiver rules, actual safety incidents, caregiver risk perception, socioeconomic stressors, sleep, physical activity, family ADHD history, initial attention traits, anxiety, and relevant developmental or learning conditions. Collect multiple reports and age-appropriate activity monitoring where feasible. Repeated measures of actual risk are needed because a reduction in independent movement can be a reasonable response to changing danger rather than an arbitrary parenting choice.

Primary analysis. Fit preregistered lagged longitudinal models addressing whether restriction at wave t predicts change in functioning at wave t+1 conditional on earlier outcomes, and vice versa. Adjust for time-varying factors; evaluate plausible family-level confounding and clustered data. Use sensitivity analyses for unmeasured confounding, missingness, attrition, and informant disagreement. When assumptions permit, inverse-probability weighting or related causal methods may be examined, while recognizing that adjustment alone cannot prove causation.

Success criterion. Evidence consistent with RASC would require a temporally ordered association that replicates across measurement methods, has clinically meaningful magnitude, and survives robust sensitivity tests. A statistical coefficient with trivial practical significance, or an effect visible only in caregiver reports, would be insufficient.

## 7.3. Study B: Factorial autonomy-by-movement mechanism experiment

Design. Approximately 240 children aged 8–12, with and without independently assessed ADHD, would complete four short, safe, counterbalanced activity sessions that vary two factors: choice (child selects an activity versus receives an adult assignment) and physical movement (active versus sedentary). Activities must be matched as closely as feasible for novelty, social interaction, intrinsic interest, difficulty, adult warmth, and time. The proposed sample is provisional and would require a power analysis that accounts for within-child correlation and crossover effects.

Measurements. Obtain immediate ratings of restlessness, enjoyment, perceived autonomy, and state anxiety before and after each condition, followed by a blinded sustained-attention task and independent behavioral coding. Assess historical restrictions and expectations separately, without creating rule-violation scenarios or threatening punishment. Analyze the autonomy main effect and autonomy-by-history interaction with hierarchical models; evaluate activity effects as a rival explanation. Use intention-to-treat classification for assigned session conditions and report condition fidelity.

Ethics. No child is confined, deceived about danger, ordered to break rules, or punished. The laboratory tests immediate effects of choice and movement. It does not directly reproduce the feared consequences described in the original observation. That ethical limitation narrows the strength of inferences about threat coupling, which should be measured through safe, naturally occurring reports.

## 7.4. Study C: Active-control intervention trial

Design. Enroll children with persistent attention difficulties and independently measured histories of reduced autonomy. Randomize an eight-week program to (i) supported self-directed exploration and play or (ii) equally intensive and physically comparable guided activities with more adult-selected structure. Ensure both groups receive safe, beneficial activities. Preserve ordinary clinical care, including prescribed medication, across both arms, and document any treatment changes.

Endpoints. Prespecify teacher-rated attention and everyday impairment in a setting outside the intervention as co-primary or hierarchically ordered outcomes, with independent objective attention performance, anxiety, and child-reported agency as secondary outcomes. A three-month post-intervention follow-up should evaluate durability. Intervention personnel cannot be blinded, but outcome assessors should be masked wherever feasible, and families should be given equivalent expectations of benefit.

Inference. An increase in enjoyment alone would support the value of autonomy but would not demonstrate a clinically useful attention intervention. The key test is whether the choice-rich program improves meaningful functioning more than a matched activity program, especially among a subgroup identified with independent pre-randomization RASC measures. Define subgroup criteria and interaction tests in advance. Final sample size should follow realistic minimally important differences and attrition estimates, not an arbitrary headline target.

## 7.5. Ancillary adult study

A separate adult observational sample could explore whether recollections of confinement, relief from self-directed activity, and punishment expectancy relate to present-day context sensitivity. Retrospective childhood exposure measures are vulnerable to memory reconstruction, current-symptom contamination, and social desirability. Such work should be labeled exploratory and should never be substituted for the prospective child cohort when making causal claims.

# 8. Statistical and causal-identification commitments

Preregistration should specify one central effect per study before analysis: in Study A, the prospective effect of restriction on later real-world impairment after prior impairment is included; in Study B, the incremental immediate effect of choice above movement on restlessness; in Study C, the between-arm difference in functioning at the primary endpoint. Secondary outcomes and interaction tests require multiplicity control and transparent reporting. Report confidence intervals, missing-data assumptions, robustness checks, recruitment flow, protocol deviations, and all prespecified negative results.

Covariate decisions should follow an explicit causal graph rather than a kitchen-sink regression. Some candidate variables, such as physical activity, may be mediators of restriction effects as well as confounders, depending on timing. Naively adjusting for a mediator can erase the very pathway under investigation. Genetic liability is imperfectly captured by family history; molecular measures may be informative with separate consent and appropriate ethical oversight. Comparing siblings, where feasible, may reduce shared-family confounding but will not eliminate child-specific unmeasured causes.

The paper distinguishes statistical association from clinically meaningful change. Positive findings across one or more studies would support specific links, but they would not automatically demonstrate the entire chain from childhood restriction to adult ADHD-like impairment. Evidence for mediation requires appropriate temporal measurement, careful assumptions, and replication. An overall RASC classification should be deferred until the individual components show acceptable reliability and predictive validity.

# 9. Rival explanations and hard tests

## 9.1. Reverse causation

Children with high activity levels may be supervised more closely or restricted because caregivers reasonably anticipate risky behavior. This path predicts that early impulsivity and safety incidents precede increased restrictions. Study A directly tests both directions. Failure to separate them invalidates a causal conclusion.

## 9.2. Pure exercise, novelty, or social-contact effects

Outdoor freedom usually changes several things at once: movement, light exposure, peers, novelty, and voluntary choice. A child’s relief outside may derive entirely from the first four. Study B separates movement from autonomy, and future designs could independently vary novelty and social contact. If perceived choice adds no effect, RASC must narrow accordingly.

## 9.3. Generalized anxiety and conditioned fear

Fear after unauthorized exploration might simply reflect normal concern about consequences; heightened fear may also reflect generalized anxiety or previous punishment unrelated to attention symptoms. RASC therefore predicts a distinct temporal pattern rather than treating all post-escape anxiety as evidence. A viable account must show something beyond ordinary fear of being caught.

## 9.4. Baseline ADHD, sleep, learning differences, and household stress

ADHD may coexist with anxiety, insufficient sleep, learning difficulties, and socioeconomic adversity. Family restrictions could correlate with these factors without causing the measured attention outcomes. Careful clinical ascertainment and multiple informants are essential. The model should not be used to tell an individual that a clinician’s ADHD diagnosis was incorrect based solely on a remembered childhood experience.

## 9.5. Cultural stereotypes and legitimate protection

Social context can shape children’s opportunities without implying pathology. Safety-driven supervision may prevent harm. The hypothesis concerns measurable individual restrictions, desired activities, realistic risks, and learned consequences; it makes no claim that a country, ethnicity, or parenting culture has one uniform childhood pattern. Investigators must distinguish protectiveness from coercion and avoid moralizing caregiver decisions under threat.

# 10. Prespecified falsification and theory revision

The RASC hypothesis should be considered substantially weakened if one or more of the following emerge consistently in high-quality replication studies:

- Restriction does not precede changes in symptoms or functioning after measurement of initial attention traits, genuine safety risks, and major confounders; instead, symptoms consistently precede restriction.

- The apparent calming effect of autonomy disappears after physical movement, novelty, and engagement are experimentally matched.

- The proposed relief-then-fear sequence is absent in prospective data or occurs equally often when no rule is perceived to have been broken.

- Choice-rich intervention fails to outperform a physically matched and expectancy-matched program on independently assessed, meaningful functioning.

- RASC indicators cannot be measured reliably, generalize poorly across settings, or yield no out-of-sample predictive improvement over baseline ADHD, anxiety, and sleep.

Negative findings should trigger narrowing or rejection, not the invention of unobservable conditioning mechanisms after results are known. Conversely, a positive choice effect in a short experiment would show that choice matters in the moment, not that childhood confinement caused ADHD. A causal developmental modifier claim requires longitudinal triangulation, mechanistic evidence, and clinically relevant intervention results.

# 11. Potential clinical and public-health implications

If future research supports RASC, assessment could incorporate a brief developmental history of independent play, mobility restrictions, source of restrictions, rules governing exploration, and activities that reliably calm restlessness. Treatment research might test autonomy-supportive behavioral programs as an adjunct to established ADHD care. This would be most relevant when the characteristic context-dependent pattern is present; it would not justify universal claims or a new diagnosis without validation.

In practice, safe autonomy is constrained by age, disability, geography, and neighborhood conditions. Family and community interventions could aim to expand developmentally appropriate choices without exposing children to preventable danger. Strengthening accessible recreation, supervised-but-self-directed play, and predictable nonthreatening boundaries may be beneficial even if RASC ultimately fails as an ADHD-specific explanation. These possible applications remain proposals, not recommendations derived from trial results presented here.

The model supplies no basis for stopping prescribed stimulant or nonstimulant medication, rejecting professional assessment, or characterizing ADHD as merely a habit. Any individual treatment changes should be made with a qualified clinician. RASC is also not a substitute for evaluating trauma, anxiety, or other conditions when clinically indicated.

# 12. Limitations, novelty, and publication status

The core idea originates in a single retrospective narrative. The paper provides no original datasets, patient recruitment, statistical analysis, effect-size estimate, clinical validation, or replicated observations. Its proposed sample sizes are design illustrations, not formal power calculations. The model’s components overlap established theories of autonomy, reinforcement learning, childhood adversity, fear, parenting, and exercise. Its candidate contribution is the specific temporally ordered combination of restriction, compensatory stimulation seeking, self-directed relief, and punishment-linked fear, plus a research design that isolates voluntary choice from physical movement.

Originality is provisional. This manuscript is a targeted conceptual synthesis, not a systematic novelty review. Further literature searching, expert review, protocol refinement, patient and caregiver input, ethics approval, and empirical tests would be required before submitting causal or clinical claims. Distinctive terminology must earn its place through predictive value. If established constructs explain the same data equally well, the RASC label should be discarded.

# 13. Conclusion

Repeated constraints on a child’s ability to move, explore, and choose may alter the circumstances in which that child learns to regulate stimulation. When unauthorized exploration offers immediate relief followed by fear of punishment, a recognizable context-dependent response pattern may develop. The Restricted Autonomy–Stimulation Conditioning hypothesis translates that intuition into operational measures, competing causal pathways, disconfirmable predictions, and an ethically feasible research agenda.

The decisive research question is whether this sequence predicts later attention impairment beyond pre-existing ADHD traits, exercise, sleep, anxiety, family circumstances, and real-world danger. The hypothesis merits testing precisely because its proposed components can be separately measured and because its failure conditions are explicit. Whether it becomes a useful developmental modifier model depends entirely on evidence not yet collected.
